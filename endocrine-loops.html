The endocrine backbone that runs under every MS2 exam & all of maternity

The Four Loops — Quick Reference

Loop 1 — The Master Loop
Learn this one first. It is the grammar of the whole system. Once you can draw it, every “is this primary or secondary?” question answers itself.
LOOP 1

Hypothalamus → Pituitary → Gland → Feedback

MS2 Exam 1MS2 Exam 2MS2 Exam 5Maternity
Why it earns its keep: every gland in the body runs on this exact shape. Thyroid, adrenal, ovary, testis. So you are not learning five systems, you are learning one pattern five times. The lab logic is the payoff — you can tell primary from secondary without memorizing a single disease.
A patient has a low T4 and a high TSH. Where is the problem, and what is the disease?
tap to check yourself
The thyroid gland itself — this is primary hypothyroidism. The pituitary is working fine and is screaming for more thyroid hormone (high TSH), but the gland cannot answer, so T4 stays low. If the pituitary were the problem, both would be low.
Why does giving a patient prednisone for months shut down their own adrenal glands?
tap to check yourself
Negative feedback. The body senses plenty of steroid on board, so the hypothalamus and pituitary stop sending CRH and ACTH. With no signal arriving, the adrenal cortex goes dormant and shrinks. Stop the drug suddenly and there is no cortisol from either source — that is an adrenal crisis. This is why steroids are always tapered.
Loop 2 — The Mirror Pairs
Never study these one at a time. Study them as opposites and you cut the memorizing in half — if you know one side cold, the other is just the flip. Tap each to open.

Cushing’s vs Addison’s
CORTISOL · adrenal cortex
TOO MUCH — Cushing’sMoon face, buffalo hump, truncal obesity with thin limbs, purple striae, high glucose, high sodium, low potassium, hypertension, thin skin, bruising, infection risk.TOO LITTLE — Addison’sWeight loss, fatigue, bronze skin, low glucose, low sodium, high potassium, hypotension, salt craving. Crisis = shock. Treat with fluids, steroids, glucose.
Graves’ vs Myxedema
T3 / T4 · thyroid
TOO MUCH — hyperthyroidEverything speeds up: weight loss despite eating, heat intolerance, diarrhea, tachycardia, tremor, insomnia, exophthalmos. Crisis = thyroid storm (fever, tachycardia, delirium).TOO LITTLE — hypothyroidEverything slows down: weight gain, cold intolerance, constipation, bradycardia, dry skin, hair loss, fatigue, depression. Crisis = myxedema coma (hypothermia, hypoventilation).
SIADH vs Diabetes Insipidus
ADH · posterior pituitary
TOO MUCH ADH — SIADHWater is held in. Urine is scant and concentrated, blood is diluted: low sodium, weight gain, no edema, confusion, seizures. Treat with fluid restriction.TOO LITTLE ADH — DIWater pours out. Huge volumes of dilute urine, raging thirst, high sodium, dehydration, weight loss. Treat with fluids and desmopressin.
Hyper vs Hypo Parathyroid
PTH · calcium
TOO MUCH PTHHigh calcium. Bones ache and fracture, kidney stones, constipation, lethargy and confusion. “Stones, bones, groans, moans.” Muscles go slack.TOO LITTLE PTHLow calcium. Muscles get twitchy and irritable: tingling around the mouth and fingers, cramps, positive Chvostek and Trousseau signs, tetany, laryngospasm. Often follows thyroid surgery.
Acromegaly vs Dwarfism
GROWTH HORMONE · ant. pituitary
TOO MUCH GHBefore the growth plates close = gigantism. After they close = acromegaly: enlarging hands, feet, jaw and facial features, deepening voice, rings and shoes stop fitting.TOO LITTLE GHShort stature with normal body proportions in a child. Caught on growth charts — which is exactly why the peds chapters drill percentile tracking.

HOW TO STUDY THESE

Fast becomes slow, high sodium becomes low, gain becomes loss.Watch outPotassium and sodium often flip opposite to each other, not with each other. Cushing’s = high Na, low K. Loop 3 — The Fluid & Pressure Axis
If you only have time for two loops, make it this one and Loop 1. This single chain explains hypertension drugs, heart failure, shock, cirrhosis ascites, AKI, CKD, SIADH and DI.
LOOP 3

RAAS + ADH

MS2 Exam 2MS2 Exam 4MS2 Exam 5Maternityevery exam, honestly
Why it earns its keep: the drugs are named after this diagram. ACE inhibitors block the lung step. ARBs block angiotensin II at the receptor. Spironolactone blocks aldosterone, which is why it is potassium-sparing. Once you see the chain, the pharmacology stops being a list and becomes a map.
Why does a patient in heart failure retain fluid, even though they are already overloaded?
tap to check yourself
Because the kidney does not measure total fluid, it measures flow past itself. A failing heart pumps weakly, so kidney perfusion drops and the kidney concludes the body is dry. It fires renin, aldosterone holds sodium and water, and the overload gets worse. The system is doing its job with bad information — and that is exactly why we block it with ACE inhibitors.
Your patient is on spironolactone. Which lab do you watch, and which direction?
tap to check yourself
Potassium, watching for it to go high. Aldosterone normally dumps potassium in the urine. Block aldosterone and that dumping stops, so potassium climbs. Teach the patient to avoid salt substitutes, which are usually potassium chloride.
A head-injured patient suddenly puts out 6 liters of very dilute urine. What is happening?
tap to check yourself
Diabetes insipidus from injury to the posterior pituitary or hypothalamus. Without ADH the kidney cannot hold water. Expect dilute urine with low specific gravity, rising serum sodium, and dehydration. Replace fluids and give desmopressin. The mirror of this — SIADH — is just as common after head injury, so read the sodium before you decide which one it is.
Loop 4 — The Steroid Profile
Not a gland, a drug side-effect pattern — but it behaves like a loop and it is worth points on four different exams, because half the patients in this curriculum are on steroids for something.
LOOP 4

Long-term corticosteroids

MS2 Exam 2MS2 Exam 3MS2 Exam 4MS2 Exam 5
Why it earns its keep: this is Loop 1 in real life. The drug is the “final hormone,” feedback silences the hypothalamus and pituitary, and the adrenal gland goes dormant from disuse. Every one of those side effects is just cortisol doing its normal job at an abnormal dose.
Why is masked fever the most dangerous item on that whole list?
tap to check yourself
Because steroids suppress the inflammatory response that produces the fever. So the patient can have a serious infection with a normal temperature and no redness or swelling. You have to watch for subtler cues — a change in mental status, low-grade tachycardia, a rising white count, malaise — instead of waiting for the classic signs. This is a very common priority question.
A patient on chronic prednisone is admitted for surgery. What does the body need that it cannot make?
tap to check yourself
Extra cortisol for the stress response. Normally the adrenals would surge cortisol to handle surgery, but they are suppressed. Without supplemental “stress-dose” steroids the patient can drop into adrenal crisis: hypotension, weakness, hypoglycemia, shock. Same principle applies to trauma, serious infection, and childbirth.
