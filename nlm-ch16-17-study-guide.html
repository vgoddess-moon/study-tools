Professor-Emphasized Concepts 4 topics

+
DEFINITELY ON THE EXAM
Innate (Non-specific) vs. Adaptive (Specific) Immunity
"I want to bang this into your head between the specific and non-specific."
Innate (Non-Specific): You are born with it. It does not have memory.
Lecture Analogy: If someone tells you to "count every car" on the road, that's non-specific. It reacts to any invader the same exact way.
Examples: skin, mucous membranes, inflammation.
Adaptive (Specific): You adapt to it. It has memory (memory B cells).
Lecture Analogy: If someone tells you to "count only the red cars," that is specific. It requires a previous exposure to create a specific antigen response.
DEFINITELY ON THE EXAM
Inflammation DOES NOT Equal Infection
Professor's Rule: Inflammation is non-specific. You can have inflammation without an infection!

Lecture Examples: A bee sting, getting hit with a hammer, or getting a charley horse all cause inflammation, but they are NOT infections.
The 5 Cardinal Symptoms of Inflammation & Why They Happen
  • Redness & Warmth: Caused by blood rushing to the area (vasodilation).
  • Swelling: Caused by the extra blood and fluid gathering.
  • Pain & Decreased Function: Swelling puts pressure on nerves and restricts movement.
Types of Acquired Immunity
Natural Active: You get sick, your body fights it, you recover (e.g., getting the flu from a dorm roommate). Lasts a long time.
Artificial Active: Vaccines. You are "stuck with a needle" with a fake/weak virus to trigger antibody production.
Natural Passive: Antibodies passed from mom to baby (breast milk/placenta).
Artificial Passive: Injected antibodies (e.g., Tetanus shot).
"Please remember that part... passive immunity provides only immediate short-term protection" because your body didn't do the work.
Visual: HLA Barcodes
Human Leukocyte Antigens are proteins on your cells. The immune system scans them like a grocery store barcode. If it reads "self," it leaves it alone. If the barcode doesn't match perfectly, it attacks it as "non-self."
Visual: Antigen-Antibody Lock and Key
Lecture Analogy: Insulin acts as a key to open the cell door for glucose. Similarly, antibodies act as specific keys for specific antigen locks. If your body builds a key for "COVID A," that key perfectly locks and neutralizes COVID A. It might slightly fit "COVID B" if it hasn't mutated much, but it won't work at all for "COVID C."

Fast Rules 6 rules

+
Fast Rule
If you see LEFT SHIFT (Bandemia) → think Severe Infection/Sepsis. The bone marrow can't keep up and is dumping immature neutrophils (bands) that can't fight effectively.
Fast Rule
If you see HIGH EOSINOPHILS → think Parasitic infection or severe allergic reaction/asthma.
Fast Rule
If you see LOW ANC (Absolute Neutrophil Count) → think Immunocompromised. The patient is at high risk for fatal infection.
Fast Rule
If you see IgE → think "Eek! Allergies" (histamine release).
Fast Rule
If you see IgG → think "General & Gone" (past infection/long-term immunity).
Fast Rule
If you see IgM → think "Me first" (new/recent infection).

Common Exam Traps 2 traps

+
Exam Trap
Trap: Thinking older adults will always show a high fever with infection.
Reality: Older adults have a diminished immune response and may not mount a fever or typical leukocytosis; minor infections can quickly become undetected sepsis.
Exam Trap
Trap: Assuming passive immunity lasts forever.
Reality: Passive immunity is temporary because the host didn't make the memory cells.

Chapter 17: Allergy and Immunity Conditions

+

Key Vocabulary 4 terms

+
Anaphylaxis
ana- = against/backward · -phylaxis = protection
Widespread, life-threatening type I hypersensitivity reaction involving blood vessel dilation and bronchoconstriction.
Angioedema
angio- = vessel · edema = swelling
Severe type I reaction causing deep-tissue swelling of the face, lips, tongue, and neck.
Autoimmunity
auto- = self
Inappropriate immune response where antibodies attack the body's own healthy tissues.
Retrovirus
retro- = backward
A virus (like HIV) that uses RNA and the enzyme reverse transcriptase to hijack a human cell's DNA.

Professor-Emphasized Concepts 4 topics

+
DEFINITELY ON THE EXAM
Anaphylaxis First-Line Action
Professor's Rule: The absolute FIRST intervention for anaphylaxis is assessing the airway and giving intramuscular epinephrine.

Epinephrine immediately constricts blood vessels, improves cardiac output, and relaxes bronchioles.

Do not give antihistamines first!
Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE) Management
  • Major fatal complications are kidney failure (lupus nephritis) and cardiac tissue damage.
  • Sun exposure induces flare-ups; patients must avoid the sun.
  • A classic sign is the "butterfly rash" across the nose and cheeks.
HIV Infection Stages & Teaching
Stage I (Acute): Initial symptoms are vague, presenting as flu-like symptoms (fever, sore throat, swollen lymph nodes) so people rarely suspect HIV.
Stage III (AIDS): Diagnosed when CD4 count is < 200 or the patient develops an opportunistic infection (e.g., Kaposi sarcoma, pneumocystis pneumonia, candidiasis).
Transmission: Via blood, semen, vaginal secretions, and breast milk. Education on safe sex is imperative, even if using PrEP/PEP, as drug resistance can occur.
Transplant Rejection Timing
Hyperacute: Minutes to hours. Must remove the organ immediately.
Acute: 1 week to 3 months. T-cells and NK cells actively attack. Treated with medications to save the organ.
Chronic: Months to years. Leads to scarring/fibrosis and eventual loss of organ function.

Hypersensitivity Types Chart

+
Type Name Mechanism Timing Examples
Type I Immediate / Allergic IgE binds to mast cells, causing sudden histamine release. Minutes to < 1 hour Peanut allergy, bee sting, anaphylaxis, asthma, allergic rhinitis.
Type II Antibody-Mediated / Cytotoxic Autoantibodies attack self-cells or foreign cells. Hours to days Blood transfusion reactions, hemolytic anemia.
Type III Immune Complex Antigen-antibody complexes form and lodge in small blood vessels (causing vasculitis). 4–10 days Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE), Rheumatoid Arthritis.
Type IV Delayed (Cell-Mediated) Sensitized T-cells release mediators and trigger macrophages. No histamine involvement! Delayed: 12–72 hours Poison ivy (contact dermatitis), TB skin test, sarcoidosis.

Fast Rules 4 rules

+
Fast Rule
If you see ACE Inhibitors (blood pressure meds) → think Angioedema risk. Bradykinin build-up can cause severe lip/facial swelling even years after starting the drug.
Fast Rule
If you see flu-like symptoms + high risk behavior → think Stage I Acute HIV.
Fast Rule
If you see tick bite + bull's-eye rash → think Lyme Disease. Lyme is an exaggerated immune response, not an autoimmune disorder.
Fast Rule
If you see long-term steroid use → think Osteonecrosis (bone death) and increased risk for severe infections.

Common Exam Traps 3 traps

+
Exam Trap
Trap: Thinking Lyme disease is autoimmune.
Reality: It causes a massive inflammatory/immune response, but the antibodies target the spirochete (the tick bacteria), not your own tissues.
Exam Trap
Trap: Treating a Type IV Delayed Hypersensitivity (like Poison Ivy) with antihistamines.
Reality: Type IV is mediated by T-Cells, not histamine (IgE). Corticosteroids are required for relief.
Exam Trap
Trap: Assuming antiretroviral therapy (ART) kills HIV.
Reality: ART only controls replication/reduces viral load; it does not kill the virus.

Medication Quick Reference 4 meds

+
Epinephrine
First-line for anaphylaxis. Relaxes bronchiolar smooth muscle and constricts blood vessels to counter histamine.
Corticosteroids (Prednisone)
Used for SLE flares and severe allergic reactions. Do not stop abruptly (causes adrenal crisis). Long-term use causes bone necrosis and immunosuppression.
Hydroxychloroquine
Modifies immune response in SLE. Adverse effect check: Instruct patients to report vision changes/get eye exams.
Antiretroviral Therapy (ART)
Combination therapy for HIV to prevent resistance. Must monitor Liver Function Tests (LFTs) because the liver metabolizes most of these drugs. Strict adherence is required.
